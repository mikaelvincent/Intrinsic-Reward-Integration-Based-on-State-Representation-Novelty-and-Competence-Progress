### 2.1 Intrinsic Motivation in Reinforcement Learning

Intrinsic motivation in reinforcement learning refers to internally generated reward signals that complement environment reward during policy optimization {{CIT:3,20}}. In sparse-reward tasks, these auxiliary signals were used to encourage exploration before reliable extrinsic feedback became available {{CIT:11,12}}.

Early formulations described intrinsically motivated behavior as a mechanism for seeking experiences that improve predictive competence rather than only maximizing immediate external payoff {{CIT:17,20}}. Later surveys and taxonomies grouped intrinsic methods into families such as novelty seeking, prediction-error curiosity, information gain, and competence or progress based objectives {{CIT:1,11,12}}.

Within deep reinforcement learning, intrinsic rewards were commonly implemented as additive shaping terms combined with extrinsic reward and scaled by a task-dependent coefficient {{CIT:6,13,15}}. This formulation was practical and effective in many benchmarks, but it did not generally preserve policy invariance because the intrinsic term can alter which trajectories are preferred during optimization {{CIT:9}}. For that reason, intrinsic methods were typically evaluated empirically through sample efficiency, asymptotic return, and reliability across seeds {{CIT:15,18,19}}.

The literature established two relevant observations for this study. First, intrinsic rewards can produce substantial gains in early exploration and learning speed {{CIT:6,13,15}}. Second, intrinsic objectives may remain active in regions where surprise stays high but useful learning has saturated, creating a mismatch between exploration pressure and task progress {{CIT:8}}. These observations motivated the present focus on progress-sensitive intrinsic shaping.
